SELL Expected : 

5% haircut on gain/loss
realized = current_wealth × (1 + unrealized_gl × 0.95)
the sale, bid-ask spreads, market impact, and partial fills mean you realise only 95% of that floating gain.

Transaction Costs:
sell_start = realized × (1 − 0.05)
Approx ~5% 
This deduction comes off your entire realised position, so it's a larger absolute hit for bigger or longer-held positions.

Behavioral Penalty:
sell_alloc = stock_alloc × 0.30
Investors who sell during volatility rarely re-enter at the same equity exposure. They instead move to 30% of their original stock allocation, rest invested in bonds, cash


Why the gap grows with time
Both penalties compound exponentially. The transaction cost is a one-time multiplicative hit (~−5%), but the allocation shift reduces your growth rate for every single year of the horizon. Over 10 years, a portfolio growing at 9% (blended 70% equity) vs ~7.5% (blended 21% equity) creates a massive terminal wealth gap — not because of any single year, but because the compounding differential accumulates relentlessly.



Table:

You're accepting a much wider distribution of outcomes in exchange for a higher expected mean. Whether that's rational depends entirely on:

Your time horizon — longer horizons shift the crossover point lower (bad markets recover)
Your need for the money — if you need this capital in 2 years, P10/P25 outcomes are very real risks
Your actual behavioural response — the SELL model assumes you stay at 30% equity; if you'd re-enter at full allocation after a year, the SELL penalty shrinks

In the bottom 25% of scenarios — i.e., bad markets — selling actually leaves you richer. This makes intuitive sense: the 5% exit cost and behavioural re-allocation penalty hurt less than a large equity drawdown does in a bear market. The bond-heavy SELL portfolio acts as a shock absorber.



India VIX:

India VIX (India Volatility Index) is a real-time, NSE-calculated index that measures the market’s expectation of volatility over the next 30 days. Often called the "fear gauge," it indicates investor sentiment and potential risk, rising during uncertainty and falling during stable periods, using NIFTY 50 options data.Key Aspects of India VIX:Measurement: It calculates expected annual volatility in percentage terms based on the order book of NIFTY options.Significance: Higher VIX indicates fear or high anticipated volatility, while lower VIX suggests stability and investor confidence.Usage: Investors and traders use VIX to assess risk, manage portfolios, and set option premiums, as high volatility usually leads to costlier options.Common Ranges: VIX generally stays in the 15–30 range, but it can spike to 90+ during crises like the 2008 crash or 2020 pandemic.Relationship with Nifty: India VIX often shares an inverse relationship with the Nifty index; as the market drops sharply, the VIX rises.

\chapter{Literature Survey}
In this chapter, we present the theoretical background and the literature behind Folium. For each of the sources mentioned below, we state their well-established contributions, drawbacks, and applicability to our project.

\section{Behavioral Foundations and Observed Investor Actions}
The Prospect Theory of Kahneman and Tversky \cite{b1} tries to explain the anomalies in expected utility theory in risky situations. Some of the main components of the Prospect Theory include reference point-based evaluation, asymmetry in gains and losses, and nonlinear probability weightings. This theory attempts to explain why an investor acts as if the decision depends on the loss in relation to the price paid for the investment, even when the decision should have been based on the prospective value of the investment. This is a descriptive model of decision making rather than a method of categorizing a situation in the real world. For Folium, it explains why the investor should consider unrealized gains and losses along with the risk context as possible signals; however, this theory cannot be used to conclude that all loss-making investments are irrational. The Cumulative Prospect Theory \cite{b17} introduced cumulative probability weighting and explained the fact that choice in the context of uncertainty differs from the maximization of expected value.

In Odean's paper \cite{b2}, trading data at brokerage firms are examined in order to see if investors cash out their winning stocks more easily than losing stocks. This analysis connects the behavioral finance theory to real-world data and proves that the disposition effect can be quantified using trading data and not just self-reporting. The paper does not provide a decision-time classifier, personal intervention, or a multi-decade counterfactual simulator. Folium uses the return and the context of a position as model features; without complete trading data, one single decision is only an indicator of the disposition effect. Shefrin and Statman \cite{b4} link the behavior of selling winners and holding losers to mental accounting, regret aversion, and self-control, while Thaler's mental accounting \cite{b18} helps understand how gains and losses are mentally accounted for. This helps explain why the same situation of wealth can have different meanings for different people, but it does not help in creating a multi-class classifier that can be tested against market paths. Folium uses some of these ideas in a quantitative way using portfolio features.

Sequential decisions may bring about informational cascades in the sense that people may behave rationally by doing what the prior individuals have done, despite having information that indicates an action contrary to those taken previously (Bikhchandani et al., 3)\cite{b3}. Thus, going along with the herd is not always irrational since some information is conveyed through social interactions. Although this model specifies under what circumstances a cascade occurs, it still fails to determine an individual's intention at a certain time from market information. In the work by De Long et al., it was shown that the presence of rational arbitrageurs does not rule out the existence of price influence caused by noise traders  \cite{b19}, which means that the measures of the crowd are pertinent to the market but does not prove that a certain person is engaging in herding. Hence, Folium makes use of social measures as proxies for herding. The discussion of efficient market theory by Fama provides the required perspective on the matter \cite{b20}.

However, Barber and Odean \cite{b14} point out that individuals who trade actively will have a tendency to generate smaller net profits. In another study \cite{b15}, Barber and Odean establish overconfidence in terms of trading activity when considering gender difference. Group-level trends such as these do not serve as diagnostics on an individual level. Folium does not base its approach on any demographic generalizations for that reason. In turn, Grinblatt and Keloharju \cite{b16} argue that various traits of investors, experience, and the trading environment affect decisions. Thus, Folium faces a similar constraint.

\section{Machine Learning for Financial Classification}
Gradient boosting is described by Friedman \cite{b26} as the process of optimizing a function stage-by-stage. Chen and Guestrin \cite{b5} present XGBoost, a gradient-boosted tree method which includes regularization, sparsity-aware split finding, and scalable approximate learning. The advantage of XGBoost on tabular financial datasets comes from its ability to learn non-linear dependencies between unrealized return, market volatility, liquidity, and investment horizon without any assumption of linear decision boundaries. Random forests, proposed by Breiman \cite{b25} as an ensemble of randomized decision trees, serve as a strong non-linear benchmark to compare XGBoost on the same partitions of the data, while the literature does not require boosted trees to outperform forests on artificial behavioral labels. Folium utilizes this family of algorithms on a multi-class task where label quality and probability calibration are as important as accuracy. Winning on synthetic rules can just mean that the model has learned the labeling procedure.

In Zadrozny and Elkan \cite{b6}, transformations of classifier scores into probabilities are discussed. This is relevant as, when the classifier presents certainty to a user, accuracy would not allow one to know for sure whether the presented probability corresponds to frequency of its correctness. As shown by Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana \cite{b27}, discrimination and probability calibration are separate tasks and boosted trees, especially, are known to have an inclination to shift probabilities away from 0 and 1. Guo et al. \cite{b28} prove the independence of high accuracy from good calibration and propose to use expected calibration error as the evaluation measure; however, since the empirical work of Guo et al. [28] is based mainly on neural networks, none of calibration methods would be optimal for tree ensemble. Calibration error and reliability on holdout data are shown on the Folium.

Saito \& Rehmsmeier \cite{b29} show that precision recall provides more insights than ROC in case of an unbalanced dataset. Folium is a multiclass classification algorithm. However, since class imbalance makes a difference, Folium uses one-versus-rest precision and recall calculation using macro-averaging, as depending solely on accuracy may hide class problems. De Prado \cite{b24} talks about financial machine learning model evaluation practices. They involve time-sensitive data, leakage and backtesting challenges. Artificial data split cannot give an insight on generalization, hence temporal holdout is another feature of Folium algorithm.

\section{Explainable Machine Learning}
The SHAP approach of Lundberg and Lee \cite{b7} attributes a prediction to the features in relation to a background distribution, and Lundberg et al. developed an accurate and efficient method for tree ensembles \cite{b35}. In Folium, SHAP tells us whether the behavioral reading was driven by the unrealized loss, crowding, or liquidity aspect. The problem with SHAP is that it explains the prediction function and not what would happen if a feature were manipulated in reality, and the output depends on the correlation between the inputs. Janzing et al. \cite{b31} present the notion of feature relevance as causal and stress the difference between the observational contribution and the interventional contribution. This is why Folium views SHAP as predictive attribution, but causal claims require a graph.

Ribeiro et al. \cite{b8} present LIME, a technique for locally approximating the black box with a simpler model. The quality of this approach relies on perturbation sampling and building of the neighboring area. Folium uses tree-specific attributions, therefore, no need to fit a local surrogate, yet the explanations should be tested numerically and linguistically. Counterfactual explanations by Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell \cite{b9} give examples of input perturbations which will lead to a different prediction, and Mothilal, Sharma, and Tan \cite{b30} provide a range of such perturbations. Change in the prediction does not mean action or cause-effect relationship. Folium's comparison of HOLD vs SELL is different from this: it compares two scenarios on paired market paths in response to the specific model question, not a general causal question about the investor’s future behavior. Weber, Carl, and Hinz \cite{b34} review explainable artificial intelligence in finance and point to stress testing specific to finance. Folium adds bilingual, checked facts as a design feature, although readability and completeness of explanations alone do not imply that explanations improve decision-making.

\section{Causal Modeling and Identification}
\cite{b10} Pearl constructs structural causal models through the use of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), interventions, and adjustment criteria. The major point here is that an observed relation does not become a cause-effect relation without certain assumptions about confounding and the graph structure. Folium models postulate relations between the state of the market, the portfolio, constraints on the investor, and the choice in terms of a DAG; therefore, their estimates become dependent on the graph and covariates, and they can never be considered the causal truth. G-computation was developed by Robins \cite{b36}; consistency, positivity, and conditional exchangeability assumptions are discussed in \cite{b37}.

As described by Sharma and Kiciman \cite{b11}, the DoWhy framework breaks down causal inference into four steps: modeling the graph, estimand identification, estimation, and refutation. Placebo tests, random common cause checks, and subsampling can uncover instabilities, but passing these tests does not rule out all unobserved confounders, which is especially important in the case of synthetic investor data. Double machine learning for treatment effects with flexible nuisance models has been proposed by Chernozhukov et al. \cite{b32}; however, identification relies on conditional exchangeability, and thus Folium’s synthetic causal checks cannot replace an identification design.

\section{Long-Horizon Simulation and Portfolio Choice}
The use of resampling techniques on dependent samples is done by Künsch \cite{b12}, as the independent bootstrap samples will destroy the dependency structure. This forms the basis of why block sampling is used in creating the market scenario where block sampling captures the clustering of volatility and serial dependencies more accurately than sampling independent months, but the accuracy is still dependent on the history sample period, stationarity, the block size, and the similarity between the future regime and the previous one. The stationary bootstrap developed by Politis and Romano \cite{b33} uses random block sizes and can be considered an alternative to the fixed blocks method. Bootstrap technique is formalized by Efron and Tibshirani \cite{b13} in the form of a measure of uncertainty which makes the computation of quantiles valid instead of one deterministic number. Folium plots the lower tail, median, and upper tail of the simulated wealth; the percentiles represent the behavior of the simulator, not the coverage probability of future markets. The regime switching model of Hamilton \cite{b23} warns us against returns being sampled from one single stable distribution.

Portfolio selection by Markowitz \cite{b21} is based on expected return, variance, and diversification, which means that the decision needs to be analyzed in terms of the risk of the whole portfolio and not of a single investment. Merton \cite{b22} considers lifetime portfolio selection in an uncertain environment, proving the importance of horizon, consumption, and changing wealth constraints. Folium takes into account all these factors using scenario analysis, but does not solve the problem of optimal control.

\section{Security Foundations for a Financial Web Application}
Financial data that is sensitive in nature is processed by Folium, and the application layer complies with existing standardization criteria. JSON Web Tokens are standardized as per RFC 7519 \cite{b38}, while time-based one-time password two-factor authentication conforms to RFC 6238 \cite{b39}. Passwords are stored as bcrypt \cite{b40}, while their strength is tested by zxcvbn \cite{b41}. To prevent cross-site request forgery, the double-submit cookie technique as specified by OWASP \cite{b42} is used. Postgres database provides support for the per-user isolation by implementing PostgreSQL row-level security \cite{b43}. The tamper-proof audit trail mechanism uses hash-chaining as suggested by Haber and Stornetta \cite{b44} using SHA-256 \cite{b45}\cite{b46}.

\section{Research Gaps Addressed by Folium}
The reviewed literature on behavioral finance, portfolio theory, calibrated machine learning, causal identification, time series resampling, and explainable AI consists of different but complimentary approaches. Five gaps emerge based on the literature. First, tools and research in this field usually regard all losses on the sale or sale of a winner too soon as a bias; however, a decision-time tool should account for forced sales, rebalancing, and short-term objectives as rational. Second, classifiers that are proved to be used by end users rarely indicate whether their confidence is reliable. Third, feature attributions are often treated as causal factors. Fourth, simple “what if” calculators compare actions under different random scenarios or ignore taxes, thus adding noise and overstating the need for holding. Fifth, explanations provided might misrepresent numbers, and checks that deny wording with numbers not included in the facts calculated are rare.

Behavioral patterns can only be used at the moment of decision if labelling is representative and exceptions are considered. Gradient boosting allows non-linear classification, but reliability and construct validity must be handled separately. Feature attribution improves interpretability without giving causal identification, defensible causal analysis needs graphs and sensitivity analysis, long-horizon resampling must account for temporal dependence and shifts in distribution, and a fluent explanation is not necessarily faithful to the simulation behind it. Folium combines candidate behavior identification, explicit bounds, paired decision comparison and fact-backed explanation in a single decision-time process. This combination is the technical and evaluative contribution of the work; none of the individual algorithms is claimed to be newly invented.
